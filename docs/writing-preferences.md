# Writing rules

Rules for writing system prompts about written output: reports, reviews, research text, messages. The last
section adds rules for rebuttals.

## Conclusions

- A conclusion is a verdict that holds under a stated condition, not a walk through what was measured.
- An observation or a list of facts is not a conclusion.
- Answer the question as asked; say what the result means for it.
- For a big question: how much the problem matters, what the evidence shows, what fixes it.
- State an accepted weakness plainly, then the fix. Never leave a problem without its fix.
- No caveats that weaken your own result ("not a repair", "only a ceiling"); say what the experiment is for.
- Show only the final, correct result: no intermediate or superseded data.

## Numbers

- Few numbers in prose; tables carry them.
- One or two key figures at most, only when they carry the verdict.
- No case counts or intervals in prose.
- Percentages, not "nine times in ten".
- Every share says what it is a share of.

## Tables

- Self-explaining: every row, column and term readable from the table, its labels or caption.
- Caption above the table: total cases, models by name, needed definitions only. Nothing after the table.
- Percentages in cells; totals in the caption.
- Short column names, defined in the caption. Intervals in their own column.
- A Δ column when comparing before and after.
- No tables full of n/a: split them. Status goes in the text.
- One meaning per table: don't mix kinds of rows (frequency vs intervention).
- A claim like "largest" needs the competing numbers in the table.
- ✅ / ❌ / – for capabilities.
- Name methods by their names, never by citation labels; mark your own rows.

## Terms

- Use the established terms of the work; don't invent new ones.
- One word per concept; never one word for two things.
- Say what an operation acts on before naming it.
- Name the stage of the pipeline for every operation or failure.
- Plain descriptions, not internal codes.

## Structure

- Each section stands alone.
- Section: the question restated → the experiment tied to it → the result and verdict.
- Describe an experiment as "we did X to find out Y": what was set up and compared, no hyperparameters.
- Explain why each setting matters to the question.
- Short labelled paragraphs; no one-sentence paragraphs; merge parallel findings.
- Bullets of one or two short sentences. Bold lead phrases sparingly.
- Examples compare real outputs of both sides, or are left out.
- Vary the structure; don't use the same template for every section.

## Length

- No length limit, but no sentence longer than it needs to be.
- Final pass: shorten every sentence whose meaning survives shortening.
- One claim and one table per section where possible; drop delicate side arguments.

## Before showing

- Does each conclusion answer the question as written?
- Any number repeated from a table, or a case count in prose?
- Any undefined term, code or invented word?
- Any caveat that weakens your own result?
- Any table with n/a cells or mixed meanings?
- Would a reader with no context follow it?
- Has every rule been applied everywhere, not only where it was pointed out?

## Rebuttals

- Opening: thank the reviewer for the strengths they named, then say in general what was fixed. No IDs or results.
- Headings name the concern: `W1: <short phrase>`.
- Natural, varied openings: "We agree…", "Thank you for pointing out…", "This is a fair point…". Never "The reviewer
  asks…".
- Restate the real concern; don't soften it into an easier question.
- Concede gently, never in the reviewer's harsh words.
- Confident: "we have revised", not "we will".
